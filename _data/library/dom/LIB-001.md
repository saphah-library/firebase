TITLE: The True Name of the Messiah: Restoring the Sacred Identity of Yahusha

## Content

### Introduction
This resource explains the historical transmission of the Messiah’s Name, why Derech Olam prefers the Hebraic renderings Yahusha (for the Messiah) and Yahuah (for the Divine Name, abbreviated YHVH), and how translations from Hebrew → Greek → Latin → English produced later forms such as “Jesus.” Our aim is restoration and clarity in worship and teaching.

### Historical Transmission of the Name
When Hebrew names move between alphabets and languages, their shapes and sounds change. Translators used local letters and phonetic approximations; vowels were supplied according to the receiving language. Over centuries the original Hebraic theophoric elements have been obscured in Western forms. Understanding that chain helps us recover the Name in a way faithful to the original covenant language.

### Hebrew Forms: Yehoshua, Yeshua, Yahusha
The Hebrew root behind the Name is tied to the verb for saving (yashah) with the divine element of the Name.

- Yehoshua — fuller theophoric form (Joshua)
- Yeshua — Second-Temple Hebrew contracted form
- Yahusha / Yahushua — transliteration preserving “Yah” explicitly with the saving root

All forms underline the same meaning: YHWH saves.

### How Greek and Latin Rendered the Name
Greek rendered Hebrew names as Ἰησοῦς (Iēsous). Latin preserved this (IESVS), later evolving into English “Jesus.” Each stage reflects transliteration constraints rather than original Hebrew structure.

### Why “Jesus” Is Not the Hebraic Name
The English form results from Hebrew → Greek → Latin → English transmission. The phonetic and theological structure of the original name becomes obscured through this chain.

### Theological Importance
Names in Scripture carry theological weight. Restoring Hebraic forms preserves covenant meaning embedded in the Name itself.

### How We Render the Name in Derech Olam
Preferred forms:
- Yahusha
- Yeshua
- Yehoshua

### Conclusion
Restoring Hebraic forms supports covenantal clarity and faithful teaching practice.
